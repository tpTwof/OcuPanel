## Summary of Key Findings

A 62-year-old male with a 3-month history of incidentally elevated intraocular pressure presents for
glaucoma evaluation. Best-corrected visual acuity is 1.0 in both eyes. Intraocular pressure is
26 mmHg in the right eye and 24 mmHg in the left on repeated measurement, with an open angle on
gonioscopy and grade I pigmentation. Family history is positive (mother with primary open-angle
glaucoma), and the patient has a 10-year history of well-controlled hypertension.

Automated analysis of the submitted right fundus photograph reports a strong likelihood of glaucoma.
Segmentation of the optic nerve head yields a cup-to-disc ratio of approximately 0.85, with a markedly
thinned neuroretinal rim that appears narrowest inferiorly. The retinal vascular arcades show
nasalization of the vessels with kinking at the disc margin. No disc haemorrhage or marked
peripapillary atrophy is identified on this single image.

Structural and functional testing are concordant in the right eye: OCT retinal nerve fibre layer
thickness is reduced in the superior and inferior quadrants, and Humphrey 24-2 perimetry shows a
nasal step defect with MD −4.21 dB and PSD 3.86 dB. The left eye shows a normal nerve fibre layer
and no reproducible field defect.

## Diagnostic Reasoning

The combination of elevated intraocular pressure, a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.85, focal inferior rim
thinning, corresponding sectorial nerve fibre layer loss and a matching nasal step field defect
satisfies the structural–functional correlation required to support a diagnosis of glaucoma rather
than simple optic disc cupping. Physiological cupping of this magnitude is uncommon and would not be
expected to produce a congruent field defect.

The positive family history and age are established risk factors and raise the pre-test probability
substantially. The open angle on gonioscopy, together with the absence of pseudoexfoliation,
pigment dispersion, prior trauma or prolonged corticosteroid exposure, argues against a secondary
open-angle mechanism. The hypertensive history is relevant chiefly as a vascular risk factor for
progression and does not by itself explain the disc appearance.

The asymmetry between eyes — an enlarged cup and nerve fibre layer loss in the right eye versus a
normal-appearing left optic nerve — further supports a genuine glaucomatous process rather than
bilateral physiological cupping.

The findings are most consistent with **primary open-angle glaucoma of the right eye, with early
to moderate functional loss**, and a right eye that should be regarded as having established
glaucomatous damage rather than ocular hypertension alone. The left eye currently shows ocular
hypertension without detectable structural or functional damage and warrants monitoring.

## Recommendations

1. **Initiate intraocular pressure–lowering therapy in the right eye**, with a target pressure of
   approximately 30% below baseline (around 18 mmHg or lower), and consider a similarly reduced
   target for the left eye given the fellow-eye status and family history.
2. **Bilateral optic disc photography and OCT retinal nerve fibre layer** as a documented baseline,
   with repeat testing at 4 to 6 months to confirm stability.
3. **Visual field testing every 6 months** for the first year, then annually if stable, using the
   same Humphrey 24-2 strategy for comparability.
4. **Assess adherence and technique** at each visit; escalate to combination therapy or consider
   laser trabeculoplasty if the target pressure is not achieved.
5. **Coordinate with the patient's primary care physician** regarding blood pressure control,
   including avoidance of pronounced nocturnal dipping, which may contribute to optic nerve
   perfusion deficit.
6. **Counselling**: explain the chronic, asymptomatic nature of glaucoma, the importance of lifelong
   follow-up, and the fact that existing visual field loss cannot be recovered — the goal of
   treatment is to prevent further loss.
7. **Refer to a glaucoma specialist** for confirmation of the diagnosis and co-management, given
   the presence of established damage in a relatively young patient.
